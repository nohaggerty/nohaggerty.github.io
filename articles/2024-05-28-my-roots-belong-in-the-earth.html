By mile four of the hike, the scraggly low-lying bushes of desert sage and spiky bulbous giant nolina had given way to 6,000-foot-high forests of snow-laden pines, cedars, and oaks. The blue sky had transmogri fied into an expanse of dark rolling clouds, and waves of sleet started pelting our faces. The San Bernardi no National Forest rangers had warned the two of us about this storm the day before.

We pushed forward. My not-so-waterproof pants were getting drenched. Icy water dripped down my legs, pooling in my hiking boots. My torso, inside my rain jacket, was also soaked, partly from sweat. I called out to Leo and asked if we could stop for a second so I could put gloves on.

I pried my red fingers off my hiking poles. My white knuckles relaxed, but I couldn’t feel it happen. I shoved my hands into my pockets to grab gloves, but they felt more like useless jumbles of meat and bones than functioning appendages. I barely managed to get the gloves out.

I fought the cold, damp fabric, trying to wriggle my numb fingers into the bunched-up fabric, but I was shaking too much to control my hands. Leo grabbed the corner of the glove. I surrendered to his help. He looked me up and down, assessing the situation. Let’s turn around and find somewhere to set up camp for the night, he said.

Fear shot through my chest. I felt like my life was in the hands of Leo, who I had met only two months ago at a queer climbing meetup. I did my best not to let him see the panic setting in. Leo might be a seasoned outdoors expert with a deep understanding of risk in the wild, but I was not. It was my first time backpack ing, and I went in with an unearned God complex: I thought I had complete control and that nothing could take it from me — not mileage, not weight, not weather.

I met Leo shortly after arriving in San Diego for a clean energy research internship in January 2023, amid a brutal winter in California. We quickly became friends — and eventually more than friends — thanks to our mutual obsession with spending time in nature. Leo was a genderqueer trans guy who worked as an outdoor guide, taking people on trips into the wilderness kayaking, climbing, camping, and backpacking; I was a queer tree-hugger in need of a thrill. We started spending almost every second somewhere in nature — whether in the Joshua-tree-speckled Mojave desert or along the pine- and palm-covered coast with the constant smell of salt.

Now, up in the San Jacinto mountains, we were caught in one of the dozens of winter storms that closed state parks across Southern California and blanketed the region in over 20 feet of snow. It was the state’s snowiest winter since the 1950s.

We backtracked for 40 minutes, looking for a spot to set up camp. After what felt like hours, we spotted a small clearing that our tent might fit in and fought through the iced underbrush to get to the opening. As we hurled our packs over the shrubs, I slipped. An icy branch whipped back into my right eye.

Leo started unpacking tent equipment. I fought with everything I could to help, but I was shivering uncon trollably. Fear shot through me again. For the first time in my life, I felt the indiscriminate power of nature, now apathetically working against me. I’m forced to submit. Seven miles from civilization, up in the San Jacinto Mountains, without cell service, the night was approaching.

Even today, I still struggle to view that moment without the warped terror that gripped me. For Leo, the experience was much more familiar. They were already well accustomed to the fact that humans are not supreme rulers over nature. Instead, the rest of nature is our equal. As such, it demands our constant and careful consideration, both in how we treat it and how it may treat us — or else you might get caught off guard by its power and dynamism, with little ability to defend yourself.

Turns out what I jokingly called my “nature God complex” has a more technical name. When I got back to Boston after four months of living out in San Diego, I started a new job as a science journalist. I quick ly proceeded to use the job as an excuse to self-indulge, grasping at anything I could to make sense of

my experience out West. On a lazy summer day, I logged onto a meeting from the comfort of my Boston apartment with Northeastern professor John Coley to talk about his research into the psychology of peo ple’s relationships with nature. His work, with Northeastern professor Brian Helmuth, introduced me to that name: Human Exceptionalism.

It’s a trap we often fall into, living in our built environments of cities and towns (especially us Westerners). We do such a good job at creating a surface-level separation between our lives and nature, both physical ly and emotionally, that we forget we’re part of it. We start thinking that humans are exceptional in some way — fundamentally different from the other life around us. This, in turn, gives us reason to buy into the idea that we are fundamentally separate from the rest of nature, unable to affect it and immune from its power.

This feeling of separation, no matter how ordinary it feels, is dangerous. We both ignore the damage we do to the environment and the potential future repercussions of that damage. It sets us up to get blindsid ed by the inevitable damage the changing environment then does to our own health and well-being.

But our ability to recognize this is heavily tied to how much we buy into human exceptionalist thinking. Helmuth and Coley found that people who had a stronger connection with nature were better able to recognize our interdependence with nature and responsibility toward our ecosystem. And people who had fewer human exceptionalist beliefs were more willing to invest their time and money into fulfilling this responsibility.

I grew up with this feeling of separation and disconnect from nature as a fact of life. “Regular” life and outdoor life are strictly separate. It was a binary. You’re either living in the exceptional human world — in school, at work, relaxing at home, and hitting the town — or you’re taking a vacation into the “less evolved” and “wild” world. It’s not an easy worldview to shake off. Everything about modern life is de signed to reinforce it. Yet, something in San Diego broke the illusion.

***

Officially, I was road-tripping out to San Diego for a semester to do research, but secretly, I had applied to the internship to fulfill my dream of living out West. It was a deep craving that grew in tandem with my slow self-discovery of my queerness.

The more I expressed my queerness through high school, the more I was told I wasn’t welcome in the spaces I thought I belonged in — including the classroom and the lab. And so I built another binary in my life: my queer life and my “hetero” life. I was my queer self when I hung out with friends, went on dates, watched RuPaul’s Drag Race, and snuck into gay bars. I maintained my professional and hetero self

by getting good grades, expressing my interest in science, and going to a good college in Boston. Mov ing West was my escapist fantasy, and San Diego was my clever plan to satisfy both my dream and the hetero expectations I still felt: My queer self was moving out West, but my hetero self was moving for a research internship.

My first few weeks in San Diego were mundane and isolated. I didn’t talk much to my random roommates, even though my bed was in the living room. I went to work in button-ups, and on weekend nights, I went to the gay club in mesh shirts and tank tops. I’d dance with any queer person willing to hang out with a lonely East Coast transplant.

But then, on a Thursday in January, I decided to attend a meetup of the Queer Climbing Collective at a local rock gym. While bouldering with a group of fellow queers, a boy walked over to the group, his hair bouncing in sync with his flowy pants. We made eye contact; he smiled and waved. I was immediately swept away by his go-with-the-flow style, upbeat spirit, and quintessentially Californian vibe. Leo and I made plans to climb again.

The collective — and the rock gym — became the core of my San Diego social life. A week later, I reached out to Leo and a few people I met at the gym and asked if they wanted to do a little weekend hike on the outskirts of the city. They, to my surprise, instead suggested we drive two hours into the Anza Borrego Desert for the day.

The same precipitation blanketing California’s peaks with feet of snow nourished the desert soil. The water was awakening millions of flower seeds that had lay dormant under the desert surface for years, waiting for the perfect spring to sprout. So, we spent the day hiking, climbing, and admiring the wildflowers that marked the very beginning of California’s superbloom. The flowers covered the desert with splashes of pink, purple, orange, and yellow — all while they were quietly getting to work replenishing the flowers’ seed banks in the soil, gifting the desert microbes with nutritious organic matter, and cooling the entire desert.

Growing up, a trip like this would’ve been framed as a vacation away from daily life. Here, the desert excursion was simply part of regular living. It set the tectonic plates in motion. Work and my “hetero” life shrunk in significance. I’d get to the lab extra early — 6:45 a.m., right before the cafeteria opened

for breakfast — and leave around 3 p.m. I’d meet Leo outside, and we’d drive to the rock gym to meet friends.

We’d spend hours shifting between activities, squeezing all the worth out of the steep membership fee at the decked-out rock gym — playing ping pong, catching up on work, lounging, buying yerba mates, hitting the sauna (my favorite spot in the entire state), and climbing the gym’s towering 50-foot rock walls. I’d drive him home long after the sun had set, and we’d sit in his driveway chatting, waiting to see if either of us would confess the feelings we were poorly hiding.

We’d go into the wilderness with a different assortment of friends virtually every weekend — and some times even on weekdays. The separation between built life and the wilderness started to blur. My commu nity in the city was the same community I’d go outdoors with — and some of our most beloved outdoor spots were just a few minutes’ drive down the interstate.

One weekday, while Leo was obsessing over a random climb at the gym, one friend asked, “Are you two just friends or …?”

“TBD,” I said.

We grabbed dinner and sat outside. Leo looked cold. I bragged about the superiority of my East Coast cold tolerance and forced him to take my jacket. Afterward, in the car parked in front of his house, we kissed.

***

I was scared of the roots I felt quickly growing. I had promised myself to leave San Diego with no strings attached. I’d spent the past three years moving from place to place for school and internships, and I was tired of starting over. I was only going to be in San Diego for four months. The more that I let people into my life, the more it was going to hurt come May

But against my best intentions, the more time I spent with my fellow outdoorsy queers — climbing, hiking, camping, getting strip mall tacos — the deeper and quicker my relationships grew.

The community had a sublime and unspoken almost spiritual aura — one that I failed time and time again to understand. My science brain couldn’t accept that. There had to be a way to dissect it — to understand its mechanics and describe its power. So once I was back in Boston, I got to work. I obsessed over news articles, magazine pieces, and library books about the intersection of nature, community, and queerness.

One day, I was introduced to the work of Catriona Sandilands, a professor of environmental arts and jus tice at York University.

In the 1990s, she put a name to a family of new ideas that were bubbling up around gender theory and environmentalism: queer ecology. The field slowly began to consolidate, with a bold and lofty aim: under stand the science of ecology from queer people’s perspectives and, from this, develop a framework that counters the dominating — and subjective — societal framings of nature.

Not every queer person thinks alike, but our shared experiences give us a shared perspective that is pointedly different from mainstream culture. As society at large pushes queer people out of traditional spaces, it’s forced us to build our own communities and culture. Because of this, queerness has spilled into all parts of life — including our relationship with nature.

In the same way that queerness blurs the gender binary, it blurs the human-nature border, bringing us closer to nature and dissolving our notions of human exceptionalism. Understanding ourselves as part of nature allows us to recognize the queerness in our fellow living beings and appreciate the space that nature gives us to build community. In the face of violence and oppression in the built world, nature is a refuge for us to relax, have fun, heal, and build relationships.

By the time Leo and I arrived for our ill-fated late-March backpacking excursion, Idyllwild was frigid and blanketed with inches of snow. Ice crunched under the car tires as we pulled up to the U.S. Forest Service ranger station, which looks more like your friend’s grandfather’s log cabin than a government building. The sun was out, and locals filled the small downtown.

We reached the desk with two San Bernardino National Forest rangers behind it. Leo took the lead and asked which trails were open, given the heavy snowfall, and what they’d recommend. The rangers laid down a map and pointed out trails, cautioning us that another storm was coming in tomorrow afternoon.

We decided with the rangers that, instead of summiting the peak of San Jacinto, we would hike an alterna tive trail that was shorter and at a lower elevation.

Leo started filling out forms that listed our plans, where we’d park the car, for how long, its license plate, and our emergency contact. In the event our car was still in the lot unattended after we were supposed to be back, the Forest Service would know something was wrong.

One ranger left to take a phone call; the remaining ranger was soft-spoken. I wondered what her unfiltered opinion was of our plans — what she’d gossip about with the other ranger once we left. I was probably projecting my fear as the newbie in the presence of outdoor experts.

Leo and I headed into the forest to set up a hammock for lunch. We found a spot between two sturdy Coulter pines that the manzanitas hadn’t taken up yet. With smooth, orange-red strips twisting up the bark of the branches — to deter any fauna looking for food — the manzanitas dotted the snowy forest floor and looked like giant, intricate antlers sticking out of the ground.

We unpacked tortilla chips, avocados, chickpeas, berries, and Snoop Dogg wine. We ate, talked, and laughed as the hammock gently rocked in the breeze. Eventually, we settled into silence. I lay back, watching the long bendy branches of the pines dance against the sky. One dipped into the hammock, kissing my face.

It was peaceful. I looked at Leo; his eyes were closed. My heart fluttered, and I became overwhelmed with emotion. Something about it all felt so right. My relationship with Leo was supposed to be situational, pragmatic, and mutually beneficial. We were both looking for friends, and I desperately wanted to get out doors and explore California, yet I kept letting myself get more and more entangled with Leo. What to do, come May, was a problem for later. I focused on the pine branch tickling my skin.

***

I became so quickly drawn to spending time in nature mainly because it provided a space for finding peace and building relationships and community. In our built world, we’ve done a pretty good job eliminat ing places like this.

Later, my journalist investigations back in Boston led me to the idea of third spaces. It’s a term of art coined by urban sociologist Ray Oldenburg in the 1980s. Your first space is where you live; your second space is where you work. Third spaces are everything else: churches, community centers, libraries, and coffee shops. They provide essential physical spaces for communities to form and grow.

But third spaces are on the decline in urban centers. It’s a struggle I’ve faced constantly while in Boston and San Diego. Luckily, despite this, nature remains the ultimate third space — although, it’s a bit ironic to call it a third space when it’s existed long before first and second spaces were ever conceived.

Nature disarms you. It puts you in a space where it would be absurd to worry about deadlines, work logis tics, and future plans. It’s peaceful. Birds sing. There’s the most sublime mountain you’ve ever seen right in front of you. (You think this every time you see a mountain, but it doesn’t make it any less true.) Even in a howling winter storm, there’s a serenity to the situation and a simplicity to the discomfort.

In the wild, life is stripped down to the most simple truths. You need other people to survive. Once you’ve figured out food and shelter, nothing is left but to be fully and truly present with the people and nature

you’re with. Nature moves at a slower, quieter pace — once your brain slows enough to hear it, it seeps deep into your bones. With this much time to slowly and methodically and purposefully think, you get re flective, appreciative.

The science on the benefits of spending time outside — both mentally and physically — is far from ambiguous. An authoritative 2021 review from Harvard public health and medical researchers concluded there was strong evidence that spending more time in nature improved people’s blood pressure, sleep, brain function, cognitive activity, and mental health. One study discovered the simple indicator of having more birds in a neighborhood was associated with a lower prevalence of anxiety, depression, and stress. Anoth er study recruited identical twins and found that the twin who had spent more time in nature was less likely to have depression.

Queer people have historically been pushed out of many traditional spaces — from homes to schools to churches — forcing us to search for safety elsewhere. Nature doesn’t care that I’m queer. It supports me regardless. Nature itself is a lot queerer than old-school ecologists gave it credit for.

Regardless of their hypotheses or biases, scientists again and again — often accidentally — found that nature is full of messy, horny queers. One researcher, in an interview with the National Wildlife Federation, recalled studying chimpanzee behavior and witnessing one chimp named David comfort another chimp named Max with some good old-fashioned head. “I’ve never really seen a male chimp that is entirely het erosexual,” he said, bluntly.

So many species have been observed being queer and polyamorous — from mammals to fish to bugs — that it’s hardly practical to count or list them. (Though, I’ll give an honorable mention to Humpback whales: In early 2024, scientists finally witnessed, for the first time ever, two humpbacks having sex — both were male.)

Nature exists despite our urban architecture that devalues third spaces and enforces cis- and hetero-nor mative cultural rules. Nature makes itself a natural ally to queer community organizing. When I sat in my hammock, across from Leo, it almost felt as if the trees themselves were holding me — as if the trees and manzanitas and the singing birds and scurrying squirrels were nourishing the love I harbored for him.

The next day, after the treacherous first leg of our hike and amid the snowstorm in San Bernardino, Leo managed to get the tent up with minimal assistance from me, my puffy branch-whipped eye, and my cold useless hands. Our somewhat shoddy setup was vulnerable to the wind, but we were cold, soaked, and tired — it was a risk we’d take. Inside, we quickly changed into dry clothes from our packs and burrowed into warm sleeping bags. We spent the rest of the night making dinner and chatting. Our protection from the storm outside was just a thin layer of fabric, which I was reminded of every time it thrashed in a gust of wind.

The next morning, we waited for the forecasted break in the weather just before noon and cooked blue berry pancakes (divine, compared to yesterday’s lunch of salami and gluten-free crackers). Again, we talked. There’s something extremely intimate and vulnerable about being alone, far from civilization and cell service, with no one knowing your exact location.

There was a long pause in the conversation. I looked into their eyes and listened to the wind. I could sense exactly what was about to happen. “How do you feel about the word ‘love’?” Leo not-so-subtly

posed.

Being who I am, I gave Leo an esoteric rambling list of what love meant to me — my not-so-subtle way to test the waters. And when he said the three words, I said them back.

With one month left in San Diego, I had grown roots too deep into this community’s nourishing soil to ig nore, relationships too meaningful and intimate to leave. Ones that would hurt too much to cut. Ones that provided me too much of what I needed to live.

It was a powerful community, unlike anything I had felt before. These queer people had taken refuge in the wild, a space that decidedly did not care about heteronormative ideas. In this space, they had built a radical group that rejected hierarchy, strictly defined relationships, and isolationism. It blurred the lines between people and nature, the self and the group, the mind and the body. It just felt right. It nourished and protected me. It filled me with a warmth that emanated from the core of my soul. I was returning to the truth all of the living beings around me had been silently living all along.

The sky in San Bernardino had cleared up early. We dug the tent out of the foot of new snow and start ed hiking back. At one point Leo grabbed a handful of snow, broke out his raspberry beverage mix, and squirted some on. “Snowcone,” they said, motioning me to grab some snow.

I felt almost taunted by Mother Nature. The weather had been a nightmare the day before, and now it was gorgeous. It was as if Mother Nature knew what she needed to teach me, and offered up a snowcone as a gift for my open mind.

Nature is a community, in which we’re a part. Seeing this is exactly what allows us to let go of the binary view of nature and humans. Native people like botanist and professor Robin Wall Kimmerer teach us that we must listen to nature. We must receive its gifts graciously and pay them forward.

When nature seems hostile to us humans, it’s never with malicious intent — it almost always serves a pur pose for some other member of our ecosystem. Beavers need frigid temperatures that freeze over lakes, protecting their underwater dam entrances from predators. The forest needs lightning strikes to ignite the

underbrush, creating space for new saplings to grow. The ground plants need coyotes to keep the popu lations of squirrels and other plant nibblers in check. To exist in a community is to bump heads sometimes — to have interests in tension with each other.

The notion of “self-sufficiency” doesn’t exist, anyway. We do not and cannot survive in a vacuum — literal ly. Plop any organism you want into the vacuum of space or the surface of Mars, and they won’t last long (well except for you, tardigrades — never stop living your truth).

Plants are responsible for producing virtually all the oxygen we breathe, and they’re our only means to accessing the sun’s raw energy as useful calories that we can digest. The rest of the animal kingdom provides us with nutrients, too, and they care for plants that sustain us. Bees, bats, birds, and insects pol linate plants, helping them reproduce. Trillions of Earth’s tiniest living beings are constantly working in our guts to stimulate our immune system and help us process food. Sometimes it’s as simple as lemon grass gifting us with a sweet and sour taste or lichens sharing their colorful artwork on rock canvases.

These are active relationships. We cannot remove an organ and expect the rest of our body to function as normal; in the same way, we cannot sever ourselves from our responsibility to fellow life. It means we must spend our lives helping others thrive and bloom. We grew up together as a family, evolving from the

same microscopic first wonders of life. We live in the legacy of our evolutionary ancestors who made our hostile rock in space not only survivable but into a home — into the most breathtakingly beautiful, comfort able, and loving space I could ever imagine. It is now our responsibility to maintain it.

It was the morning of our last day in the Adirondacks. We had taken an eight-seater plane from Boston to my friend’s lake cabin for the week, but I felt like I still hadn’t got to know the lake well enough. It had been almost a year since San Diego, yet I was still chasing that powerful feeling of what it’s like to exist in a community with both queer people and nature. I needed to get outside again, and the canoe hanging from the shed ceiling was calling my name. I couldn’t resist the urge. Leo couldn’t either.

We wrestled the canoe down with ropes and pulleys, cleared out the leaves and cobwebs, and plopped her into the water. She was a simple Army green two-seater made entirely of wood, screws, and sealant. We got in and set off.

The wind tussled my freshly-styled hair every which way. River otters popped their heads up; birch branches kissed the water’s surface. Geese flew above us and settled down toward the cattails. Fog still nestled along the foothills; wind turbines peaked out over the horizon. We stopped paddling near the cen ter of the lake and just drifted. A familiar feeling suddenly washed over me — a sense of peace. This is it.

Since San Diego, I have shifted from scientific research to science writing and spent as much time as I could trying to make sense of it all. It allowed me, for the first time, to let myself blur the binary between my professional life and my queer life. I searched for answers in my free time and as a journalist. I inter viewed queer people and anyone who spent time outside and wrote about them.

I began noticing that the ideas of queer ecology that my San Diego chosen family had implicitly taught me weren’t queer people’s alone. Across my scattered attempts to get outside with people back on the East Coast, I watched college students find nature as a way to slow down, clear their heads, and connect with the people they love (whether they admit it or not). I saw families connect through their shared space in nature, Quakers use nature as a space to reflect. Through nature, I saw Black people build community, and environmental activists reignite their passions.

I had learned so much about the power of nature, but I longed to feel it again. While I never exactly suc ceeded in recreating what I had in San Diego in the Northeast, I still felt glimmers of it from time to time.

I felt it when I took the Amtrak to spend the summer solstice camping with a friend’s extended family. And when I spent a long weekend up in the White Mountains with my close friend from college. And when I spent the afternoon in the nearby city park reading about Alexander Humboldt in my hammock.

And now on the lake. For just a moment, I felt the true serenity of nature seep into my mind. I let the wind carry away my thoughts and the water wash my wounds. I let birds sing me songs of comfort and belong ing; otters remind me of joy. I let trees calm my breath, and the sun warm my heart. And I sat with Leo.

They brought me so far, gently and patiently introducing me to the flora and fauna, my long-lost kin. They had taken me to the woods, desert, and ocean to find myself for the very first time. In an interdependent ecosystem, forged through the act of giving, it was the ultimate gift of love.

Now, before the airport, before another terrifying eight-seater plane ride, before I returned to the grind of

Boston life, and before Leo returned to San Diego — before all of that, I had just a moment for nature to remind me who I am.
